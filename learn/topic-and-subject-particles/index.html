은/는 or 이/가? Korean Topic and Subject Particles Explained

2026-11-07 · 4 min read · ggook's studio

Two particles that both look like "the subject"

Korean marks the role of a word with a small particle after it. Two of these particles cause more confusion than any others: 은/는 and 이/가. In a simple sentence, both can appear right after the person or thing doing the action, so they look interchangeable:

  • 저는 학생이에요. I'm a student.
  • 제가 할게요. I'll do it.

They aren't interchangeable, but the difference is easier than it seems. This guide starts with the forms, then the core idea, then seven everyday situations.

Which form to use

Each particle has two forms. Choose by whether the word before it ends in a consonant or a vowel:

ParticleAfter a consonantAfter a vowel
Topic은: 책은는: 친구는
Subject이: 책이가: 친구가

A few very common words change shape before 가:

  • 저 + 가 → 제가 (I, polite)
  • 나 + 가 → 내가 (I, casual)
  • 너 + 가 → 네가 (you, casual), often pronounced 니가 in speech
  • 누구 + 가 → 누가 (who)

The core idea: topic and subject

  • 은/는 marks the topic: what the sentence is about. Think of it as "as for…". 저는 학생이에요 is "As for me, I'm a student."
  • 이/가 marks the subject: who or what does the action or has the quality, often with the focus on that person or thing. 제가 할게요 is "I'll do it", with the stress on I.

Most of the rules of thumb below follow from this one difference.

Seven situations

1. Introducing yourself and general statements: 은/는

When you talk about yourself or say something general about a topic, use 은/는:

  • 저는 캐나다 사람이에요. I'm Canadian.
  • 서울은 커요. Seoul is big.

2. New information: 이/가. Known information: 은/는

When something first enters a story or conversation, it usually takes 이/가. Once everyone knows what you're talking about, it becomes the topic and takes 은/는. Korean folk tales show this clearly:

  • 옛날에 한 왕이 살았어요. Once upon a time, there lived a king.
  • 그 왕은 딸이 세 명 있었어요. The king had three daughters.

3. Question words take 이/가, and so do the answers

Words like 누구 (who) and 뭐 (what) ask for new information, so they take 이/가. The answer fills in that new information, so it takes 이/가 too:

  • 누가 왔어요? Who came? — 민수가 왔어요. Minsu came.
  • 뭐가 제일 맛있어요? What's the most delicious?

4. Contrast: 은/는

은/는 can compare one thing with another, like "but" or "as for this one" in English:

  • 저는 커피를 좋아하고, 동생은 차를 좋아해요. I like coffee, and my younger sibling likes tea.
  • 고기는 안 먹어요. Meat, I don't eat (but other things are fine).

Notice that in the second sentence 은/는 is attached to the object, not the subject. That's why it's called a topic particle: it can mark almost anything you want to set up as the topic or contrast.

5. "It's me, not someone else": 이/가

To single out one person or thing from others, use 이/가:

  • 제가 낼게요. I'll pay (it's on me).
  • 이게 제 가방이에요. This one is my bag. (이게 is a short form of 이것이, "this thing".)

6. Two particles in one sentence

Many common sentences use both: 은/는 for the person the sentence is about and 이/가 for the thing that describes them:

  • 저는 머리가 아파요. I have a headache. (As for me, my head hurts.)
  • 저는 시간이 없어요. I don't have time. (As for me, time doesn't exist.)
  • 코끼리는 코가 길어요. Elephants have long trunks. (As for elephants, the trunk is long.)

Expressions with 있다 (to have), 없다 (to not have), 필요하다 (to need) and 아프다 (to hurt) very often follow this pattern.

7. Fixed patterns that always take 이/가

  • Before 아니다 (to not be): 저는 학생이 아니에요. I'm not a student.
  • Before 되다 (to become): 의사가 되고 싶어요. I want to become a doctor.
  • Inside a describing clause: 제가 만든 케이크 is "the cake I made". You can't use 저는 here.

Rules of thumb for beginners

  • Talking about yourself or a topic in general? 은/는
  • Asking with "who" or "what", or answering that question? 이/가
  • Mentioning something for the first time? 이/가
  • Comparing two things? 은/는
  • "I have", "I don't have", "I need", "it hurts"? Both: 저는 … 이/가

In casual speech Koreans often leave particles out altogether, as in 너 어디 가? ("where are you going?"). And if you choose the wrong one, people will still understand you: the difference is mostly about emphasis. You'll develop a feel for it by listening, so pay attention to these particles whenever you read or hear Korean.
