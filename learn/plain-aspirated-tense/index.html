Plain, Aspirated and Tense: Hearing ㄱ, ㅋ and ㄲ

2026-11-07 · 3 min read · ggook's studio

Three ways to say "k"

English has two sounds made at the back of the mouth, g and k. Korean has three, and they're written with three different letters: ㄱ, ㅋ and ㄲ. The same goes for four sets of consonants:

PlainAspiratedTense
ㄱ gㅋ kㄲ kk
ㄷ dㅌ tㄸ tt
ㅂ bㅍ pㅃ pp
ㅈ jㅊ chㅉ jj
ㅅ sㅆ ss

The difference changes the meaning of a word, so it's worth training your ear early. The tense letters are also easy to spot: they're simply the plain letter written twice.

What makes them different

Plain: light and relaxed

Plain consonants (ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅈ ㅅ) are soft. At the start of a word there's a small, gentle breath after them; between vowels they become voiced, like English g, d, b and j.

Aspirated: a strong puff of air

Aspirated consonants (ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ) come with a strong burst of air, stronger than the k in English "kite". The extra stroke in each letter is a reminder of that extra breath.

Tense: tight, with no air

Tense consonants (ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅉ ㅆ) are made with a tight throat and almost no breath, a bit like the p in "spin" or the k in "skin", but tenser. Try tightening your throat as if you were about to lift something heavy, then say the sound.

The tissue test

Hold a tissue in front of your mouth and say 파, then 빠. With ㅍ the tissue should jump; with ㅃ it should barely move. With plain 바 it moves a little.

Listen for the pitch too

In Seoul Korean, a vowel after an aspirated or tense consonant usually starts on a higher pitch than a vowel after a plain consonant. Many learners find this pitch difference easier to hear than the breath itself, so listen for it in the word sets below.

Word sets to practice

Each row is a set of real words that differ only in the first consonant. Listen to them one after another and try to repeat them.

PlainAspiratedTense
불 bul, fire풀 pul, grass뿔 ppul, horn
달 dal, moon탈 tal, mask딸 ttal, daughter
자다 jada, to sleep차다 chada, to kick; to be cold짜다 jjada, to be salty
사다 sada, to buy싸다 ssada, to be cheap

Don't worry if they sound alike at first. Most learners need some listening practice before the three become clear, and context helps a lot in real conversations.

Tense sounds you don't see in the spelling

Plain consonants sometimes become tense in speech even though they're written plain. The most common case: after a syllable that ends in a k, t or p sound, the next plain consonant is pronounced tense.

  • 학교 (school) is pronounced 학꾜, hakkyo
  • 식당 (restaurant) is pronounced 식땅, sikttang
  • 입구 (entrance) is pronounced 입꾸, ipkku

You don't need to work this out every time you read. Learn these words with their pronunciation, and many learners find the rule starts to happen on its own as they get used to saying words at normal speed.

Double consonants at the end of a syllable

Only two of the double consonants can appear at the bottom of a syllable block: ㄲ, as in 밖 (outside), and ㅆ, as in 있다 (to exist, to have). ㄸ, ㅃ and ㅉ only ever start a syllable.
