Korean Family Terms: 형, 오빠, 누나, 언니 and Beyond

2026-11-07 · 4 min read · ggook's studio

Family words do a lot of work in Korean

In English, "brother" and "sister" cover most of what you need. Korean family words are more precise: they tell you whether someone is older or younger, which side of the family they're on, and sometimes even whether the speaker is a man or a woman.

They're also used far beyond the family. As our politeness guide explains, Koreans rarely say "you" to people they respect, and family words are one of the main ways to address people warmly. So learning them is useful even if you never meet anyone's relatives.

Brothers and sisters

For an older sibling, the word depends on whether the speaker is male or female:

Who you're talking aboutIf you're maleIf you're female
Older brother형 hyeong오빠 oppa
Older sister누나 nuna언니 eonni

A trick to remember them: if you're a man, the two words you use are 형 and 누나; if you're a woman, they're 오빠 and 언니.

For a younger sibling it's simpler: everyone says 동생 (dongsaeng). To be specific, say 남동생 (younger brother) or 여동생 (younger sister). Younger siblings are usually called by their name rather than by 동생.

The general words for "brothers" and "sisters" are 형제 and 자매, and the youngest child in a family is the 막내.

Parents and children

MeaningStandardFamiliar
Father아버지아빠
Mother어머니엄마
Parents부모님
Son / daughter아들 / 딸

Children call their parents 아빠 and 엄마, and many adults keep doing so. 아버지 and 어머니 sound more formal and respectful. When you talk about someone else's parents, add the honorific -님: 아버님, 어머님.

"Our mom"

Koreans usually say 우리 엄마 ("our mom") rather than "my mom", even when talking about their own mother. The same goes for 우리 집 (our home) and 우리 가족 (our family). It isn't a mistake: family and home are thought of as shared.

Grandparents: two sides of the family

Grandfather is 할아버지 and grandmother is 할머니. To say which side they're on, Korean adds a prefix:

  • 친- for your father's side: 친할머니 (father's mother)
  • 외- for your mother's side: 외할머니 (mother's mother)

외 literally means "outside", a trace of the traditional family system in which a mother's relatives were counted as outside the father's family line. In daily life most people simply say 할머니 and add the prefix only when they need to be clear.

Uncles, aunts and cousins

This is where Korean gets precise. There are different words for your father's and your mother's brothers and sisters:

RelativeFather's sideMother's side
Uncle큰아버지 (father's older brother)
작은아버지 (father's younger brother)		외삼촌
Aunt고모이모

A father's younger brother who isn't married yet is usually called 삼촌. Their spouses have their own words too: the wife of 큰아버지 is 큰어머니, the husband of 고모 is 고모부 and the husband of 이모 is 이모부.

Counting relatives by degree: 촌

The word 삼촌 hides a neat system. 촌 counts how many steps apart two relatives are: parent and child are 1 촌, siblings are 2, an uncle is 3 (삼촌, "three steps") and a cousin is 4. That's why the word for cousin is 사촌, "four steps".

Family by marriage

A husband is 남편 and a wife is 아내. Parents-in-law have different names depending on whose parents they are:

  • A man's wife's parents: 장인 (father-in-law) and 장모 (mother-in-law)
  • A woman's husband's parents: 시아버지 and 시어머니

These are the words for talking about them. Face to face, people usually use respectful forms instead, such as 아버님 and 어머님.

Family words for people who aren't family

This is the part learners notice first in Korea. Family words are used everywhere to address people warmly:

  • 언니, 오빠, 누나 and 형 are used for older friends, classmates and colleagues you're close to, following the same male/female rule as for siblings. A woman calling her boyfriend 오빠 is very common.
  • 이모 is a friendly way to call a woman working in a small restaurant, roughly the age of your mother's sister: 이모, 여기 물 좀 주세요! ("Could we get some water here, please?")
  • 할머니 and 할아버지 are polite ways to address elderly people you don't know.

For middle-aged strangers, people also use 아저씨 for men and 아주머니 for women. The shorter 아줌마 is common but can sound less polite, so as a learner it's safer to use 아주머니, or in a shop or restaurant simply 저기요 ("excuse me").
