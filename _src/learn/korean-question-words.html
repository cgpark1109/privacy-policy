The easiest way to keep a conversation going

Being able to ask questions is one of the fastest ways to make progress in a language: every question gets you new words in the answer. Korean question words are short and easy to use, because they sit in the same place in the sentence as the answer would. If "I'm going to school" is 학교에 가요, then "where are you going?" is 어디에 가요?: just swap 학교 for 어디 ("where").

This guide uses the polite present tense from our verbs guide.

Yes-or-no questions

For a yes-or-no question you don't need a question word at all. Take a statement and let your voice rise at the end:

  • 바빠요.{{say:바빠요}} I'm busy. → 바빠요?{{say:바빠요?}} Are you busy?
  • 맛있어요.{{say:맛있어요}} It's tasty. → 맛있어요?{{say:맛있어요?}} Is it tasty?

"Yes" is 네{{say:네}} (or 예), and "no" is 아니요{{say:아니요}}. Note that 네 means "that's right", so it confirms what the other person said, rather than meaning "yes, I am": if someone asks 안 가요? ("aren't you going?") and you're not going, you answer 네, "right, I'm not".

Question words with particles

Question words take the same particles as the nouns that would answer them. A few combinations come up all the time:

  • 어디에 (where to, or where something is) and 어디에서 (where an action happens): 어디에 살아요?{{say:어디에 살아요?}} Where do you live? / 어디에서 일해요?{{say:어디에서 일해요?}} Where do you work?
  • 뭘 (short for 뭐를, "what" as the object): 뭘 먹을까요?{{say:뭘 먹을까요?}} What shall we eat?
  • 누가 ("who" as the subject): 누가 왔어요?{{say:누가 왔어요?}} Who came? Note that 누구 + 가 becomes 누가, not 누구가.

Our particles guide explains 이/가 and 은/는, which you'll also see in many questions.

몇: counting questions

몇 goes in front of a counter word, just like a number does, so the answer has the same shape as the question:

QuestionMeaningAnswer
몇 시예요?{{say:몇 시예요?}}What time is it?세 시예요.{{say:세 시예요}} It's three o'clock.
몇 살이에요?{{say:몇 살이에요?}}How old are you?스무 살이에요.{{say:스무 살이에요}} I'm twenty.
몇 명이에요?{{say:몇 명이에요?}}How many people?두 명이에요.{{say:두 명이에요}} Two people.
몇 번이에요?{{say:몇 번이에요?}}What number is it?7번이에요.{{say:칠 번이에요}} It's number 7.

Which set of numbers goes in the answer depends on the counter: native Korean for hours, age and people, Sino-Korean for numbers like bus and exit numbers. (몇 번 can also mean "how many times?", and then the answer uses native numbers: 두 번.) Our numbers guide has the details.

Asking politely

Asking someone you don't know a direct question can sound a little abrupt on its own. Two small habits make your questions sound friendlier:

  • Start with 저기요 or 실례합니다 ("excuse me") before asking a stranger.
  • When asking about someone older or of higher status, Koreans often use the honorific ending -(으)세요: 어디 가세요?{{say:어디 가세요?}} ("where are you going?") instead of 어디 가요?. Our politeness guide explains why.

Also note that some questions that sound personal in English are normal small talk in Korean. People may ask your age, because it helps them decide how to speak to you, and 밥 먹었어요? ("have you eaten?") is often just a friendly greeting, as our greetings guide explains.

Useful questions to learn first

  • 이게 한국어로 뭐예요?{{say:이게 한국어로 뭐예요?}} What's this in Korean?
  • 다시 한번 말해 주세요.{{say:다시 한번 말해 주세요}} Please say that once more. (Not a question, but you'll need it!)
  • 영어 할 줄 알아요?{{say:영어 할 줄 알아요?}} Can you speak English?
  • 이건 어떻게 읽어요?{{say:이건 어떻게 읽어요?}} How do you read this?

Putting it together
