Two sets of numbers

Once you can read Hangul, numbers are one of the first things you'll need, and Korean has a surprise: there are two complete sets of them.

  • Sino-Korean numbers (일, 이, 삼…) come from Chinese. They're used for dates, money, phone numbers, minutes and most numbers you read or calculate.
  • Native Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋…) are originally Korean. They're used for counting things and people, for hours and for age.

It sounds confusing, but there's a pattern, and the most common uses come up so often that you'll learn them quickly.

Sino-Korean numbers

The Sino-Korean system is very regular. Learn the digits from 1 to 10 and a few big units, and you can say any number.

Bigger numbers are built like a calculation, digit by digit:

  • 11 is "ten one": 십일{{say:십일}}
  • 20 is "two ten": 이십{{say:이십}}
  • 99 is "nine ten nine": 구십구{{say:구십구}}

The big units are 백 (100), 천 (1,000), 만 (10,000) and 억 (100,000,000). For 100, 1,000 and 10,000 you don't say "one" in front: 100 is just 백{{say:백}}, not "one hundred". Zero is 영, or 공 when you read out phone numbers digit by digit.

Counting in groups of four

English groups big numbers in threes (thousand, million, billion). Korean groups them in fours, around 만 (ten thousand) and 억 (a hundred million). This is the part that takes the most practice:

A handy trick: to read a big number in Korean, count four digits from the right instead of three. 50,000 becomes 5|0000, which is 오만.

Short forms before a counter

When a native number comes right before a counting word, five of them get shorter:

  • 하나 → 한: 한 명{{say:한 명}} (one person)
  • 둘 → 두: 두 개{{say:두 개}} (two things)
  • 셋 → 세: 세 시{{say:세 시}} (three o'clock)
  • 넷 → 네: 네 마리{{say:네 마리}} (four animals)
  • 스물 → 스무: 스무 살{{say:스무 살}} (twenty years old)

Telling the time mixes both

Time is the classic example: hours are native and minutes are Sino-Korean. 3:10 is 세 시 십 분{{say:세 시 십 분}}. Half past is 반, so 3:30 can be 세 시 삼십 분 or simply 세 시 반{{say:세 시 반}}.

"Number one" and "one time"

The counter 번 shows the difference nicely. With a Sino-Korean number it names something, like a numbered exit: 일 번 출구{{say:일 번 출구}} is Exit 1. With a native number it counts how many times: 한 번{{say:한 번}} means "once".

Dates

Dates use Sino-Korean numbers with 년 (year), 월 (month) and 일 (day), in that order, from biggest to smallest. November 7, 2026 is 이천이십육 년 십일월 칠 일{{say:이천이십육 년 십일월 칠 일}}.

Months are simply "number + 월", with two spelling exceptions that make them easier to say: June is 유월{{say:유월}}, not 육월, and October is 시월{{say:시월}}, not 십월.

Age

In conversation, age is counted with native numbers and 살: 스무 살 (20). On forms and in formal writing you'll see Sino-Korean numbers with 세 instead: 20세, read 이십 세.

You may have heard of "Korean age", a traditional count in which everyone is one year old at birth and gets a year older on New Year's Day. Since June 2023, South Korean law and official documents use international age, the same as most of the world, although you may still hear the traditional count in everyday conversation.

How to practice

  • Read prices out loud in Sino-Korean whenever you see them, grouping digits in fours.
  • Count everyday things around you in native numbers with 개: 한 개, 두 개, 세 개.
  • Say the time in Korean every time you look at a clock.
