What batchim is

A Korean syllable block can end with a consonant written at the bottom, like the ㄴ in 한 or the ㄹ in 글. This final consonant is called batchim (받침), which means "something that supports from below".

Almost any consonant can be written as batchim, but at the end of a syllable Korean only pronounces seven sounds. Learn which letters fall into which sound, and you can read any final consonant.

So 옷, 낮, 꽃 and 밭 all end with the same t sound, even though they're spelled with four different letters.

The k, t and p endings are unreleased: your mouth moves into position and stops there, without the little puff of air you'd hear at the end of an English word. Say "book" but stop before the k fully comes out, and you're close to how ㄱ ends 약.

Linking: when the next syllable starts with ㅇ

Why is the spelling different if the sound is the same? Because the original consonant comes back when a vowel follows. If the next syllable in the same word, or a grammar ending attached to it, starts with a silent ㅇ, the batchim moves over and is pronounced as the first sound of that syllable:

  • 옷이{{say:옷이}} (clothes + subject marker) is pronounced 오시, osi
  • 꽃이{{say:꽃이}} (flower + subject marker) is pronounced 꼬치, kkochi
  • 음악{{say:음악}} (music) is pronounced 으막, eumak
  • 한국어{{say:한국어}} (the Korean language) is pronounced 한구거, hangugeo

Linking is the single most useful pronunciation rule in Korean. It's why spoken Korean sounds so smooth: syllables flow into each other instead of stopping after each consonant.

Double batchim

Some blocks have two different consonants at the bottom, such as ㄺ or ㅄ. At the end of a word, or before another consonant, only one of the two is pronounced:

WordPronouncedMeaning

삶{{say:삶}}삼 samlife

When a vowel follows, both consonants are heard, because the second one links to the next syllable: 닭이{{say:닭이}} is pronounced 달기 and 값이{{say:값이}} is pronounced 갑씨.

Two more changes you'll hear every day

k, t and p turn nasal before n and m

When a syllable ending in a k, t or p sound is followed by ㄴ or ㅁ, the ending becomes the matching nasal sound: k becomes ng, t becomes n and p becomes m.

  • 감사합니다{{say:감사합니다}} (thank you) is pronounced 감사함니다, gamsahamnida
  • 한국말{{say:한국말}} (the Korean language) is pronounced 한궁말, hangungmal

That's why the polite ending -ㅂ니다, which you'll meet in your first week of Korean, always sounds like "-mnida".

ㅎ makes the next consonant aspirated

A batchim ㅎ followed by ㄱ, ㄷ or ㅈ merges with it into the aspirated ㅋ, ㅌ or ㅊ: 좋다{{say:좋다}} (to be good) is pronounced 조타, jota.

Don't let the rules scare you

These rules look like a lot on paper, but they all describe what happens naturally when Korean is spoken at normal speed. Read words, listen to them, and repeat them: within a few weeks you'll apply most of these changes without thinking.
