An alphabet designed on purpose

Most writing systems grew slowly over centuries. Hangul, the Korean alphabet, was designed on purpose. King Sejong the Great and his scholars created it in 1443 and published it in 1446 in a book called Hunminjeongeum, "the correct sounds for teaching the people".

Before Hangul, Koreans wrote with Chinese characters. Learning thousands of characters took years, so most ordinary people couldn't read or write. Sejong wanted an alphabet anyone could learn in days, and he succeeded: Hangul is famous for being easy to pick up. South Korea celebrates it every year on Hangul Day, October 9.

24 letters, plus a few combinations

Modern Hangul has 24 basic letters:

  • 14 consonants: ㄱ ㄴ ㄷ ㄹ ㅁ ㅂ ㅅ ㅇ ㅈ ㅊ ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅎ
  • 10 vowels: ㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ ㅣ

From these come 5 double consonants (ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ) and 11 compound vowels (such as ㅐ, ㅘ and ㅢ). That's 40 letters in total, and every one of them is built from the 24 basic ones. The original 1446 alphabet had 28 letters; four of them fell out of use over the centuries.

Letters stack into syllable blocks

The biggest surprise for new learners is that Korean isn't written in a single line of letters like English. Letters are grouped into square syllable blocks, one block per syllable. Each block has:

  1. a first consonant,
  2. a vowel, and
  3. sometimes a final consonant at the bottom, called batchim.

Take the word for the alphabet itself, 한글{{say:한글}} (Hangeul):

Where the vowel goes

The shape of the vowel decides the layout of the block:

  • Tall vowels (ㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅣ) go to the right of the consonant: 가 ga, 니 ni.
  • Flat vowels (ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ) go below the consonant: 고 go, 루 ru.
  • A final consonant always sits at the bottom: 각 gak, 곡 gok.
  • Compound vowels that mix both kinds wrap around the consonant: 과 gwa.

Inside a block you read left to right, then top to bottom. Blocks themselves are read left to right, and words are separated by spaces, just like in English.

The silent ㅇ

A written syllable never starts with a bare vowel. When a syllable begins with a vowel sound, the consonant ㅇ stands in the first position as a silent placeholder: 아 a, 오 o, 우유{{say:우유}} uyu (milk).

At the bottom of a block, the same letter has a sound: "ng", as in "sing". So 강{{say:강}} (river) is gang, and 방{{say:방}} (room) is bang.

Shapes that make sense

Hangul's letters aren't arbitrary. According to the 1446 explanation of the alphabet, the five basic consonants picture the mouth when you make each sound:

  • ㄱ (g/k): the back of the tongue rising to block the throat
  • ㄴ (n): the tip of the tongue touching the ridge behind the upper teeth
  • ㅁ (m): the closed lips, the shape of the mouth
  • ㅅ (s): a tooth
  • ㅇ (ng): the throat

Other consonants add strokes to these to show a stronger sound: ㄴ → ㄷ → ㅌ, ㅁ → ㅂ → ㅍ, ㅅ → ㅈ → ㅊ and ㄱ → ㅋ. The vowels are built from three ideas: a dot for heaven, a flat line for earth and a standing line for a person. Today the dot is written as a short stroke, as in ㅏ and ㅗ. A note on romanization

In these guides we write Korean in Latin letters using the Revised Romanization of Korean, the official system in South Korea. It's useful at the start, but try to move away from it quickly. Romanization can hide differences Korean speakers hear clearly: 어 (eo) and 오 (o) are both "o"-like to English ears, but they are different sounds. The sooner you read Hangul directly, the better your pronunciation will be.
