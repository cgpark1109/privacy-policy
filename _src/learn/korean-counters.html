Why Korean needs counters

In English you can say "three apples", but you also say "three cups of coffee" or "two pairs of socks". Korean does this with almost everything: when you count, you add a short counter word that shows what kind of thing you're counting. Our numbers guide introduced 개 and 명; this guide covers the counters you'll use most.

The pattern: noun + number + counter

The thing comes first, then the number, then the counter:

  • 사과 세 개{{say:사과 세 개}} three apples (apple + three + things)
  • 학생 다섯 명{{say:학생 다섯 명}} five students
  • 커피 두 잔{{say:커피 두 잔}} two cups of coffee

The counters for things and people in the next table use native Korean numbers, and five native numbers take a short form before a counter: 하나 → 한, 둘 → 두, 셋 → 세, 넷 → 네 and 스물 → 스무. So it's 한 개, not 하나 개. On its own, without the thing, you can just say the number and counter: 두 개 주세요{{say:두 개 주세요}} ("two, please").

A few more you'll meet: 그릇 (bowls, as in 밥 한 그릇, a bowl of rice), 켤레 (pairs of shoes or socks), 벌 (outfits and items of clothing) and 송이 (flowers).

When you can't remember the right counter for an object, 개 is the usual fallback for things, and you'll still be understood. For people, use 명, and for animals, 마리. Restaurant staff often ask 몇 분이세요?{{say:몇 분이세요?}} ("how many people?") with the respectful 분, but you answer with 명: 두 명이요.

Counters with Sino-Korean numbers

Some counters take Sino-Korean numbers (일, 이, 삼) instead, mostly for money, time, dates and numbered things:

CounterUsed forExample
원won (money)오천 원{{say:오천 원}} 5,000 won
분minutes십 분{{say:십 분}} ten minutes
인분servings (of food)삼겹살 이 인분{{say:삼겹살 이 인분}} two servings of pork belly
층floors삼 층{{say:삼 층}} the third floor
번numbers (bus, exit)일 번 출구{{say:일 번 출구}} Exit 1

Notice that 분 appears in both tables: with a native number it counts people respectfully (두 분, "two people"), and with a Sino-Korean number it counts minutes (이 분, "two minutes"). Our numbers guide covers 년, 월 and 일 for dates, and our days and dates guide shows how to ask for the date.

Native Korean numbers only go up to 99, so from 100 up the hundreds are Sino-Korean, even with counters like 명: 백 명{{say:백 명}} (100 people).

Asking how many: 몇

Put 몇{{say:몇}} ("how many") in front of the counter:

  • 몇 개 드릴까요?{{say:몇 개 드릴까요?}} How many would you like?
  • 가족이 몇 명이에요?{{say:가족이 몇 명이에요?}} How many people are in your family?
  • 몇 살이에요?{{say:몇 살이에요?}} How old are you?

Asking someone's age is common in Korea, because it affects how people speak to each other, as our politeness guide explains. With someone much older, such as an elderly person, use the respectful 연세가 어떻게 되세요?{{say:연세가 어떻게 되세요?}} (연세 is the respectful word for age); with other adults you don't know well, 나이가 어떻게 되세요? is common.

Putting it together

직원: 어서 오세요. 몇 분이세요?{{say:어서 오세요. 몇 분이세요?}}
Staff: Welcome. How many people?

손님: 세 명이요.{{say:세 명이요}}
Customer: Three.

직원: 이쪽으로 앉으세요.{{say:이쪽으로 앉으세요}}
Staff: Please sit over here.

손님: 김밥 두 줄하고 라면 한 그릇 주세요. 콜라도 한 병 주세요.{{say:김밥 두 줄하고 라면 한 그릇 주세요. 콜라도 한 병 주세요}}
Customer: Two rolls of gimbap and a bowl of ramyeon, please. And a bottle of cola, too.

줄 ("line, row") is the counter for gimbap rolls. Try counting the things on your desk with the right counters, or see all guides.
